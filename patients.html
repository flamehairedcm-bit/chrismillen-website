What happens

From referral to back with your dentist

Your own dentist stays your dentist. I see you for the specialist part of your care.

  1. Your dentist refers you

    Your dentist sends a referral to the practice. You can also refer yourself: how to get a referral.

  2. We get in touch

    You'll receive a letter to say you've been referred. Our referral coordinator then contacts you, explains the process and the cost, and books your appointment. You'll get written confirmation, details about the practice, and a reminder before you come.

  3. Your first visit

    We talk about your medical history and what's bothering you, and I examine your mouth. Most importantly, we talk about what matters to you.

  4. A planning visit

    Often we book a second visit to take everything needed to understand your mouth fully: photographs, a scan of your teeth, x-rays and a 3D scan where needed, and a full gum assessment.

  5. Your written report

    I send you a detailed report of what I found, what it means, and your treatment options, with costs. Your dentist receives a copy too. I encourage you to talk it over with them.

  6. Treatment, then back to your dentist

    If you go ahead, we plan treatment around you. When it's complete, I write to your dentist and you return to them for your routine care, with reviews here at the right times.

Your choice is always respected.

Explained simply

Four things patients often ask me about

Gum disease, and how it's treated

Gum disease (periodontitis) is an inflammatory condition. Plaque at the gum line sets off the body's defences, which over time can damage the gum and bone that support the teeth. Treatment starts with refining how you clean, then cleaning below the gum so it can heal. We then reassess and plan any next steps together.

Smoking and diabetes. Gum disease is linked with smoking and with diabetes. The European guideline for treating gum disease recommends support to stop smoking, and to control diabetes, for people having gum treatment (Sanz et al. 2020).

Vaping. The British Society of Periodontology has summarised the evidence in Is vaping harmful to oral health?, written by Dr Richard Holliday of Newcastle University (December 2023). In short: vaping is far less harmful to the mouth than smoking, but it isn't risk-free. The risk of gum disease appears lower than with smoking but higher than in people who do neither, and the long-term effects aren't yet known. Its advice is that vaping can help smokers to stop, but people who don't smoke shouldn't start. If you smoke or vape, tell us and we'll talk it through.

If you'd like help to stop smoking, the NHS service Quit Your Way Scotland is free.

Replacing a missing tooth: your options

There are usually four or five options. Sometimes leaving the gap is acceptable, though not at the front, and neighbouring teeth can move. A removable denture can replace several teeth but relies on the other teeth staying healthy. A bridge is fixed to the teeth either side. An implant stands on its own, like a screw in a wall, so it doesn't harm the neighbouring teeth; it needs enough strong bone, which we check with a 3D scan. In some cases, braces can close the gap.

A broken or heavily filled tooth

A tooth with large fillings can be protected with a crown or an onlay. A crown covers the whole tooth, like a beanie hat. An onlay covers only the part that needs it, like a flat cap. Because an onlay removes less of your own tooth, the tooth is often easier to restore again in the future.

Worn, chipped or failing teeth

When teeth are worn, chipped or have a lot of older dental work, I look at the whole mouth and how your teeth meet, then plan treatment in stages. Before we start, a mock-up made in the laboratory lets you see and try the planned result on your own teeth.

Specialists explained

What is a specialist?

A specialist is a dentist whose skills, knowledge and experience in a particular area have met the conditions of the General Dental Council, the UK's dental regulator, to join its specialist list. Only dentists on a specialist list can call themselves a specialist.

Your general dentist looks after your day-to-day dental health. A specialist has completed further training in one area and concentrates on it. Your dentist may refer you when treatment needs a specialist's assessment or planning, such as implants, gum disease, or several problems that need to be looked at together.

Common questions

Questions patients ask

Do I need a referral to see Chris Millen?

Most patients are referred by their own dentist, but you can also refer yourself. The practice explains how on its website, and the team at Edinburgh Dental Specialists can help on 0131 225 2666.

What does a specialist in restorative dentistry, prosthodontics and periodontics do?

Restorative dentistry covers rebuilding and replacing teeth, prosthodontics covers crowns, bridges, dentures and implant teeth, and periodontics covers the gums and the bone that support the teeth. Chris Millen is on the General Dental Council's specialist lists for all three.

Will I go back to my own dentist?

Yes. Chris Millen does not take on general patients. After treatment he writes to your dentist with a summary, sees you for reviews when needed, and you return to your own dentist for routine care. Your choice is always respected.

How much will treatment cost?

The cost of your first appointment is explained when the practice contacts you. After your planning visit you receive a written report with your treatment options and the cost of each, so you can decide in your own time.

Does smoking or vaping affect gum disease?

Gum disease is linked with smoking, and the European guideline for treating gum disease recommends support to stop smoking for people having gum treatment (Sanz et al. 2020). On vaping, the British Society of Periodontology's 2023 summary of the evidence is that it is far less harmful to the mouth than smoking but not risk-free: the risk of gum disease appears lower than with smoking but higher than in people who do neither.
